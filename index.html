Command a real habitat beyond Earth. Manage oxygen, water, food and power, shield your crew from radiation, and handle emergencies — day by day. Every choice has a cost. Every system matters.

🎮How to Play

  1. Choose a mission: Lunar Outpost or Martian Outpost.
  2. Read your mission briefing, then Begin Mission.
  3. Each day you have 3 action points — spend them on life support, repairs, crops and medical care.
  4. Press Advance Day to consume resources and face what the frontier throws at you.
  5. Emergencies require decisions — read the consequences before you choose.
  6. Survive all 7 days with your crew healthy to complete the mission.

Learning objectives

  • How life-support systems recycle air and water
  • Why power is the backbone of every habitat system
  • How radiation shapes habitat design on the Moon and Mars
  • The trade-offs commanders face when nothing is unlimited

🌍About This Project

ASTRO COMMAND was built for the NASA Space Apps Challenge. It is an educational simulation: all resource numbers, consumption rates and event outcomes are simplified game settings invented for play — they are not real NASA operational limits.

The science behind the systems — air, water, food, radiation and power — is real, and every module links to official NASA research in the section.

                ACADEMY LIBRARY

                Learn: Living Beyond Earth

                Simple explanations of the real science behind the game — with official NASA references.

                🛠️Life-Support Systems (ECLSS)

                Astronauts cannot carry enough air, water and food for a long mission, so spacecraft recycle them. This recycling system is called ECLSS — Environmental Control and Life Support System. It cleans the air, manages pressure, controls temperature and removes CO₂ so the crew can breathe safely.

                NASA: Environmental Control and Life Support Systems →

                💧Oxygen Generation

                The space station makes oxygen by running electricity through water (electrolysis), splitting it into oxygen and hydrogen. The oxygen goes into the cabin air; the hydrogen is vented or reused. This is why power and water are linked to breathing — just like in the game, where boosting oxygen costs power.

                NASA: Oxygen generation in ECLSS →

                ♻️Water Recycling

                On the International Space Station almost nothing is wasted: sweat, humidity from breath, and even urine are captured, filtered and turned back into clean drinking water. Recycling recovers a very large share of the water on board, so a habitat that recycles well needs far fewer water deliveries.

                NASA: Water recovery systems →

                🌱Space Agriculture

                NASA studies growing crops such as lettuce, tomatoes and dwarf wheat in space using water-based and air-based techniques instead of soil, with special LED lighting. Fresh food improves nutrition and crew morale, and plants help refresh the air. In the game, tending the greenhouse trades water and power for food.

                NASA: Space Crops research →

                ☢️Radiation Protection

                Space radiation comes from the Sun (solar particle events) and from outside the galaxy (galactic cosmic rays). Unlike Earth, the Moon and Mars have almost no atmosphere or global magnetic field to block it. Too much radiation raises the risk of cancer and damages the body, so habitats need thick shielding and storm shelters.

                NASA SRAG: How space radiation affects humans →

                ⚡Power Management

                Every life-support system — oxygen generators, water recyclers, greenhouse lamps, heaters and comms — runs on electricity. Solar panels provide it, but nights, dust and failures cut the supply. A commander must always keep a power reserve, because without power, air and water stop flowing.

                NASA: Powering life-support systems →

                🌕Moon vs Mars: Why They Differ

                The Moon is close (about 3 days of travel) but has two-week nights with no sunlight, extreme temperatures and abrasive dust. Mars is farther away, with dust storms, a thin CO₂ atmosphere and weaker sunlight for solar power — but it has seasons, water ice and a day length similar to Earth's. Both worlds demand recycling, radiation shielding and careful power use.
